1. Cleaning and aligning the data

CGMs report every 5 minutes, fingersticks a few times a day, and exports often have gaps. Readings are linearly interpolated onto a 15-minute grid. If two readings are more than 60 minutes apart, the gap is left empty rather than invented. Values are stored in mg/dL. CSVs labelled mmol/L are multiplied by 18, and CGM “Low”/“High” markers become 40 and 400.

3. The forecast: four models compete

No single model is best for everyone, so HealthMap fits several and lets your own history decide.

A. Personal regression (ARX-style ridge regression)

A separate linear model for each horizon (15, 30, … 120 minutes) predicts how much glucose will change from:

  • Recent momentum: change over the last 15, 30 and 60 minutes.
  • Time of day: encoded as sin/cos so 11:59 PM and 12:01 AM are neighbors.
  • Daily rhythm: how far you are from your usual level for the target time.
  • Logged events: each meal, activity, or insulin dose is passed through an action curve (rises, peaks around 60–75 minutes, fades) of the same family as the “carbs on board” and “insulin on board” curves used in insulin pumps. The model learns how strongly each type moves your glucose.
Δglucose(t → t+h) = w₀ + w₁·Δ15 + w₂·Δ30 + w₃·Δ60 + w₄·sin(tod) + w₅·cos(tod)
                  + w₆·meal_effect(h) + w₇·activity_effect(h) + w₈·insulin_effect(h)
                  + w₉·(typical(t+h) − now)

Weights are fit by ridge regression, which is least squares with a small penalty that keeps the model from over-reacting to noise when data is limited. This family (autoregressive models with exogenous inputs, “ARX”) is one of the most studied approaches in glucose-prediction research and the standard baseline that fancier models are compared against.

B. Smoothing + daily rhythm (damped-trend Holt)

ℓₜ = α·yₜ + (1 − α)·(ℓₜ₋₁ + φ·bₜ₋₁)          level
bₜ = β·(ℓₜ − ℓₜ₋₁) + (1 − β)·φ·bₜ₋₁         trend (φ < 1 makes it fade)
forecast(h) = w·[ℓ + (φ + … + φʰ)·b] + (1 − w)·typical(t+h),   w = e^(−h/τ)

Damped-trend smoothing is a long-established general forecasting method. The blend toward your daily rhythm is HealthMap's own addition. α, β, φ and τ are chosen from 108 combinations by grid search on your data.

C. Ensemble

The average of A and B. Averaging different models is one of the most reliable findings in forecasting research, because their errors partly cancel.

D. “No change” baseline

Assumes glucose stays where it is. If a model can't beat this, it isn't adding anything. It's shown on the scoreboard for honesty.

How the winner is chosen

Your last 30 days are split in time order: models learn on the first 60%, the winner is picked on the next 20%, and every model is scored on the final 20%. Splitting by time (never shuffling) matters, because otherwise a model could “peek” at the future. The chosen model is then refit on all your data for live forecasts.

The shaded range and low-risk estimate

The purple band is the 10th–90th percentile of past forecast errors at each horizon, so roughly 8 times in 10 the real value landed inside it. The “chance of going below 70” is the share of past forecasts whose actual outcome, applied to today's forecast, would have dipped under 70 within 2 hours.

4. Insights & suggestions

The Insights tab runs a set of transparent rules over your last 14 days: time in range vs target, week-over-week change, overnight lows, early-morning rises, the 3-hour window with the most highs, weekday vs weekend differences, and variability. For logged meals and activities, it measures the peak change within 3 hours and the change at 2 hours, then groups them by name so you can compare “oatmeal” with “cereal”. Suggestions are phrased as things to try or to discuss with your care team, never as instructions.

5. How well-tested is this?

  • The building blocks are established. ARX/regression models, exponential smoothing, forecast ensembles, time-ordered validation, and AGP-style metrics are all standard.
  • This exact combination hasn't been clinically validated. It was developed and checked against simulated CGM data, where it consistently beats the no-change baseline by about 25–40% at 60 minutes. Real data is noisier, so expect larger errors.
  • Your scoreboard is the real test. The numbers above are measured on your own held-out data, not on the simulator.
  • What would be better still: validation on a public research dataset (e.g. OhioT1DM), and physiological models that use meal carbohydrate amounts and insulin doses directly. Those need data most people don't log.

6. Limitations

  • Unlogged meals, stress, illness, and hormones are invisible to the model and are the biggest sources of error.
  • Sparse fingerstick data can't support momentum, so forecasts fall back mostly to your daily pattern.
  • Meal response numbers are associations: a walk after dinner, or a correction dose, changes what follows.
  • CGM sensors themselves have ~9–10% error and lag blood glucose by 5–15 minutes.

HealthMap is a learning and visualization tool, not a medical device. Never dose insulin or treat a low based on a forecast. Confirm with your meter and follow your care team's plan.
